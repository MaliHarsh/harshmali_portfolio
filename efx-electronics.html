01 / System architecture

Separate decisions from motor power.

The ESP-WROOM-32 development board sits at the control layer. Its job in the documented architecture is to coordinate commands and interfaces, while dedicated drivers provide the electrical power needed by the motors. This distinction allows operator intent—drive, move the belt, actuate the claw—to be translated into different forms of motion.

02 / Controller input & software organization

Turn a command into predictable behavior.

The control architecture has two useful software responsibilities: interpreting operator input and converting that input into actuator commands. Keeping those responsibilities separate makes it easier to isolate whether unexpected motion originates in the input, command mapping, or driver interface.

1. Interpret the inputRead the operator’s requested motion, reject stale input, and account for small joystick offsets with a deadband.
2. Form a motion requestConvert drive and steering intent into left/right commands, then keep both within a valid range.
3. Command each actuatorUse the appropriate interface for each mechanism: DC drive, stepper pulses, or servo commands.
4. Handle loss of controlDefine a neutral response when input disappears or a fault is detected, instead of retaining the last motion request.

For a two-motor chassis, a useful reference mapping is left = drive + turn and right = drive − turn, followed by normalization. That illustrates how steering can be separated from motor wiring.

03 / Actuator behavior

Different mechanisms need different commands.

Belt: repeatable increments

The stepper-and-driver pair provides a way to express belt travel as a sequence of commanded increments. Pulse count relates to commanded travel, while pulse rate relates to commanded speed. Translating those values into belt motion also requires the pulley geometry and microstep setting. Commanded steps alone do not prove that the belt reached its destination: binding or a load beyond the motor’s capability can cause missed motion.

The DRV8825 notes identify direction, step, microstepping, enable, reset, sleep, and fault functions. Current-limit setup must use the carrier’s actual sense resistance. The driver’s fault output is a local status signal; a robot-wide stop requires additional system logic.

Chassis: load and driver selection

With two drive motors, the electrical selection should follow the mechanical demand. Starting, turning, and a stalled wheel can demand more current than free running. The L298N listing and the “smaller” TB6612FNG option therefore represent a design decision to resolve using motor requirements, supply voltage, heat dissipation, and mounting space—not size alone.

Claw, sorting, and chute

Servo control must match the intended behavior: positioning a claw and continuously driving a mechanism require different command interpretations. The color sensor introduces a measurement step before sorting correction. The solenoid and vibration motors add separate switched loads. Their interfaces need suitable drive circuitry rather than direct power from a controller output.

These mechanisms also create sequencing questions: when should the belt move, when should the claw act, and when is a color reading meaningful? Explicit operating states would help prevent commands from conflicting while material is being transferred.

04 / Circuits & power distribution

Make the electrical interfaces explicit.

Circuit documentation connects each component to a clear power and signal plan. A component inventory tells a reviewer what exists; an interface record explains how the pieces can operate together.

  • Power paths: identify the main supply and which loads use a regulated output. The LM2596 provides step-down conversion; its output should be matched to the loads on that rail.
  • Signal compatibility: confirm logic levels and module pinouts before connecting ESP32 outputs, sensor lines, and driver inputs. A board label alone does not establish every interface voltage.
  • Ground and routing: maintain the necessary shared signal reference while planning motor-current return paths and keeping sensitive wiring away from moving mechanisms and noisy power wiring.
  • Switched loads: select suitable switching stages and inductive-load protection for the solenoid and vibration motors. Document the selected circuit rather than assuming every load connects like a servo.
  • Serviceability: label connectors, provide strain relief, and leave access to adjustments and terminals so troubleshooting does not require dismantling the robot.
05 / Mechanical–electrical integration

Why this mattered to my manufacturing role.

My role centered on manufacturing, assembly, and the drivetrain. The electronics architecture gives context to that work: a motor mount must support both the motor and its cable exit, the frame must accommodate drivers and their connections, and moving belt or claw components must not pull on wiring.

This is where subsystem ownership becomes system awareness. A mechanically compact layout can still be difficult to assemble or service if connectors are blocked. A drivetrain load can also change what is required of the driver and power system. Understanding those interfaces helps explain how my build work connects to the team’s broader design.

My contribution: manufacturing, assembly, and the drivetrain—work that connects the team’s electrical architecture to the physical robot.
06 / Validation approach

Build confidence one interface at a time.

Reference validation sequence / A structured approach to reviewing each subsystem and its interfaces.

  1. Confirm the hardware: record the actual motor-driver choice, supply settings, servo model, pin assignments, and connector map.
  2. Check command interpretation: inspect neutral, forward/reverse, steering, and input-loss behavior with actuator outputs disabled.
  3. Test individual actuators: verify direction, belt motion, claw behavior, and switched-load operation separately under controlled conditions.
  4. Introduce representative loads: observe current draw, supply stability, heating, and mechanical binding during realistic operation.
  5. Integrate the sequence: check that material transfer, sensing, and sorting commands agree with the physical mechanism.
  6. Record outcomes: capture expected behavior, observed behavior, and the adjustment made. This turns debugging into a reusable engineering record.

What a complete handoff should contain

A final package would connect the electronics list to a wiring schematic, a controller-input map, a driver configuration table, and short subsystem test records. Together, these records connect a parts list to a robot whose behavior can be understood and reproduced.
